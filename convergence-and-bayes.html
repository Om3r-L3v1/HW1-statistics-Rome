§1The four modes

A random variable is a function of the outcome ω. "Xn → X" can mean several things depending on whether we look at every ω, at the probability of being far away, at an average distance, or only at the distributions.

ModeDefinitionWhat it says
Almost sure
Xn → X a.s.		P( {ω : Xn(ω) → X(ω)} ) = 1Take one outcome ω and look at the whole sequence of numbers X1(ω), X2(ω), …: it converges in the ordinary sense, for all ω except a set of probability 0. A statement about each path.
In mean square (L²)
Xn → X in L²		E[(Xn − X)²] → 0The average squared distance goes to 0. Large deviations are allowed only if they are rare enough that their squares still average out. Needs finite second moments.
In probability
Xn →P X		P(|Xn − X| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0For each fixed large n, being far from X is unlikely. It says nothing about the same ω at different n: a path may still jump away infinitely often, at times that become rarer.
In distribution
Xn ⇒ X		Fn(x) → F(x) at every point x where F is continuousOnly the distribution functions converge. Xn and X do not even need to live on the same space; the values of Xn and X need not be close at all.

How the modes are related

Solid arrows always hold. Dashed red arrows do not hold in general; the counterexamples are in §2.

Why the arrows hold. L² ⇒ P follows from Chebyshev's (Markov's) inequality: P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xn − X)²]/ε². a.s. ⇒ P because, if almost every path eventually stays within ε, the probability of being outside at time n goes to 0 (dominated convergence applied to the indicator). P ⇒ distribution because if Xn is close to X with high probability, P(Xn ≤ x) is squeezed between P(X ≤ x − ε) and P(X ≤ x + ε), up to small terms. One partial converse: convergence in distribution to a constant c implies convergence in probability to c.

    The same modes in statistics

    • Consistency of an estimator θ̂n means θ̂n → θ in probability (strong consistency: a.s.). The sample mean, the sample variance and maximum-likelihood estimators are consistent under mild conditions, by the LLN.
    • Asymptotic normality means √n(θ̂n − θ) ⇒ N(0, v), a convergence in distribution. It gives standard errors, confidence intervals and tests. For the MLE, v is the inverse Fisher information.
    • Glivenko–Cantelli: the empirical distribution function converges to F uniformly, almost surely. This is why the data "look like" the population for large n.
    • Slutsky and the delta method combine the modes: if Zn ⇒ Z and Yn → c in probability, then ZnYn ⇒ cZ. This is what allows replacing σ by the sample s in √n(X̄n − μ)/s ⇒ N(0, 1).

    §4Why √n

    The √n comes from how variances add.

    Var(Sn) = Σi Var(Xi) + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj)   always
    = nσ²   if the Xi are uncorrelated (independence is more than enough)
    ⇒ sd(Sn) = σ√n,   sd(X̄n) = σ/√n,   Var(Sn/na) = σ² n1 − 2a

    The mean of Sn grows like n, but its standard deviation only like √n, because independent errors partly cancel. Dividing by n gives a variance σ²/n → 0, which is the LLN. Dividing by √n gives a variance that stays at σ², the only scaling that leads to a non-degenerate limit, which is the CLT. With any other power the limit is either a constant or does not exist.

    Independence is the condition that removes the covariance terms. If the observations are correlated, the covariances add up and the variance of the mean changes. For an AR(1) series with lag-one correlation ρ, Var(X̄n) ≈ (σ²/n)·(1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ): still a 1/√n rate, but as if we had only n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ) independent observations. With perfect correlation (all Xi equal) there is no averaging at all.

    In statistics the same fact gives the standard error σ/√n, the 95% interval x̄ ± 1.96·s/√n, and the rule that the precision only improves with the square root of the sample size: to halve the error we need four times as much data.

    §5Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

    Conditioning means restricting attention to the outcomes where B happened, and renormalising.

    In probability

    P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),  P(B) > 0
    P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)
    P(B) = Σi P(B | Ai) P(Ai)  (Ai a partition)
    P(Ai | B) = P(B | Ai) P(Ai) / Σj P(B | Aj) P(Aj)

    The last line is Bayes' theorem. It follows from writing P(A ∩ B) in two ways and using the law of total probability for the denominator. It "inverts" a conditional probability: from P(B | A), which is often easy to know, to P(A | B), which is what we want. The two are different, and confusing them is a common mistake.

    In statistics

    The unknown is a parameter θ and the evidence is the data x. The three pieces of Bayesian inference are:

    • Prior p(θ): what we believe about θ before seeing the data, from earlier studies, expert knowledge, or a deliberately vague choice.
    • Likelihood L(θ) = p(x | θ): how probable the observed data are for each value of θ. It is a function of θ with x fixed, and it is not a probability distribution over θ (it need not integrate to 1).
    • Posterior p(θ | x): what we believe after seeing the data.
    p(θ | x) = p(x | θ) p(θ) / p(x)  ∝  likelihood × prior
    p(x) = ∫ p(x | θ) p(θ) dθ only normalises

    The posterior after one batch of data is the prior for the next one, so learning is a chain of updates. With independent data the order does not matter: updating with x1 and then x2 gives the same posterior as updating with both at once.

        §8Advantages, limitations and influence on the conclusions

        Advantages

        • Prior knowledge enters in an explicit, checkable way, for example the base rate of attacks in §6. Ignoring it is exactly the base-rate fallacy.
        • The result is a probability statement about the unknown: "P(θ > 0.5 | data) = 0.03" or "there is a 95% probability that θ is in this interval", which is how people usually want to read a confidence interval.
        • Updating is sequential and coherent: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.
        • With little data, a reasonable prior stabilises the estimate (shrinkage), as with the sceptical prior for n = 2 or 3.

        Limitations

        • The prior is a choice, and with small samples it can decide the conclusion. Different priors can give different answers from the same data; a sensitivity analysis (as in the table of §7) should be part of the report.
        • "Non-informative" priors are not unique: uniform and Jeffreys priors already differ, and a uniform prior on θ is not uniform on log θ.
        • The posterior can only be as good as the likelihood. If the model is wrong (for example, alarms that are not independent), Bayes' theorem still runs but the posterior is misleading.
        • Outside conjugate pairs like Beta–Binomial the normalising integral p(x) has no closed form, and the posterior must be computed numerically (grids, MCMC).
        Link with part 1. As n grows, the likelihood becomes sharper and sharper around the true θ, its width shrinks like 1/√n, and any prior that gives positive weight near θ is overwhelmed. The posterior then concentrates at the true value and becomes approximately normal with standard deviation of order 1/√n (Bernstein–von Mises theorem). This is the Bayesian counterpart of consistency and asymptotic normality: for large samples, Bayesian and frequentist conclusions agree, and the prior only matters when the data are few.
