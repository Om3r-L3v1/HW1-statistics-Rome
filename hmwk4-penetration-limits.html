n systems, m attackers, one bell curve

Each attacker goes through the n systems one at a time and gets a score Xi on each one. The running total is a random walk. If we look at the total as it is, divided by n, or divided by √n, we get three different pictures as n grows. The total spreads out, the average settles on a single value (Law of Large Numbers), and Sn/√n keeps the same bell shape (Central Limit Theorem). How large should m be?

The m attackers are our sample. Every histogram, variance and probability below is estimated from m independent trajectories, so m decides how precise those estimates are. For the current m:

§1The model

Attacker j (j = 1 … m) goes through the systems i = 1 … n in order and scores Xi(j) on each. All the scores are independent and have the same distribution.

  1. One attacker, one trajectory. After k systems attacker j has the score Sk(j) = X1(j) + … + Xk(j). Plotting Sk against k gives one sample path, where k is the number of systems attacked so far.
  2. n is the system size. The number of systems is the length of the walk. The limit theorems are about what happens to Sn, Sn/n and Sn/√n when n → ∞.
  3. m is the number of attackers. The attackers are m independent copies of the same walk. For a fixed n their m final scores are a sample from the distribution of Sn, and all histograms, variances and probabilities are computed across attackers.
model ABernoullisuccess or failure

Xi = 1 if attacker j gets into system i and 0 otherwise, with P(Xi = 1) = p. Sn is then the number of systems breached, which is Binomial(n, p), and Sn/n is the attacker's success rate.

model BRademachergain or loss

Xi = +1 when the attacker gains ground on system i and −1 when it is detected and pushed back, each with probability ½. This is a symmetric random walk with mean 0, so there is no drift, only fluctuation. Because the mean is 0, the CLT applies directly to Sn/√n.

QuantityMeanVarianceBernoulli as n → ∞Rademacher as n → ∞

These follow from independence and a finite variance. E Sn = nμ by linearity, and Var Sn = nσ² because independent terms have zero covariance. Dividing by a constant c multiplies the variance by 1/c², which gives the other two rows.

    §4Law of Large Numbers: Sn/n → μ in probability

    For every ε > 0, P(|Sn/n − μ| ≥ ε) → 0 as n → ∞. We estimate this probability as the share of attackers whose average is at least ε away from μ, and compare it with the exact binomial value and with Chebyshev's bound.

    Why it converges. Chebyshev's inequality for the average gives P(|Sn/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(Sn/n)/ε² = σ²/(nε²), and this goes to 0 for any fixed ε. This is the proof of the weak law. The bound is far from tight (the real probability drops much faster, as the solid lines show), but it only needs the variance to be finite.

        Why Bernoulli needs centring. In the Bernoulli model Sn/√n has mean p√n, which grows without limit (third row of §3, and the third chart of §2 with the Bernoulli model selected).
